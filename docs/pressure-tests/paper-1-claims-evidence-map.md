## Claim 1: "Discovery is already intervention"

**Exact proposition:** The consultant's presence and questions change the organizational system before any recommendations are made.

**Embedded assumptions:**
- Organizations are observer-sensitive systems (like quantum systems or social systems subject to Hawthorne effects)
- Questions constitute an intervention, not neutral data-gathering
- The change produced by observation is non-negligible in consulting contexts
- This applies regardless of consultant intent

**Supporting evidence:**
- **Hawthorne effect literature** [1]: Individuals modify behavior in response to awareness of being observed. Original studies (1924-1932) showed productivity changes when lighting was altered—but critically, productivity also changed when conditions returned to baseline, suggesting observation itself was the variable.
- **Trial effect in medical research** [1]: Clinical trial participants show better outcomes beyond the intervention itself, attributed to "slightly better care, slightly better compliance/adherence, and selection bias."
- **Secondary observer effect** [1]: Researchers working with data impact results through analytical choices, suggesting observation affects even non-human subjects indirectly.

**Contrary evidence / Counterexamples:**
- **Original data reanalysis** [1]: Levitt & List (2011) reanalyzed original Hawthorne illumination data and found "slight evidence for the Hawthorne effect over the long-run, but in no way as drastic as suggested initially." Jones (1992) similarly found weak effects.
- **Wickström & Bendix (2000)** [1]: Argue the Hawthorne effect is "ambiguous and disputable" and recommend introducing specific psychological and social variables instead of invoking a generic observer effect.
- **Adair (1984)** [1]: Argued the effect is actually Orne's "experimental demand effect"—participants interpret the situation and respond to perceived expectations, not observation itself. This suggests the mechanism is more specific than "observation = intervention."
- **Parsons (1974)** [1]: Argued participants had access to performance counters and were responding to feedback, not observation per se.

**Boundary conditions:**
- Effect appears stronger in short-term studies; Clark & Sugrue (1991) [1] report novelty effects cause ~30% SD rise decaying to <1% variance after 8 weeks.
- May not apply when observation is routine or expected (e.g., regular audits, established metrics).
- Effect size varies by context: stronger in healthcare hand hygiene studies [1], weaker in original industrial data.

**Competing explanations:**
1. **Feedback mechanism**: Participants improve because observation provides performance feedback, not because they are observed [1].
2. **Motivation to please experimenter**: Demand characteristics, not observation itself [1].
3. **Novelty effect**: Temporary improvement from change itself, not observation [1].
4. **Management attention**: Mayo's interpretation—workers responded to sympathetic supervision, not observation [1].

**What would falsify:**
- Systematic studies of consulting engagements showing no difference in organizational behavior between announced vs. unannounced discovery phases.
- Evidence that organizations with routine external auditing show no reactivity to new consultants.
- Demonstration that question-asking without follow-up produces no measurable system change.

**Residual unknowns:**
- **Magnitude in consulting contexts**: No direct studies of Hawthorne-like effects specifically in organizational consulting discovery phases.
- **Duration**: Unknown how long observer effects persist in organizational settings after consultant exits discovery phase.
- **Mechanism**: Unclear whether effect operates through demand characteristics, feedback, motivation, or genuine system perturbation.

**Verdict:** **Partially supported but mechanism unclear.** Observer effects are real in some contexts but the original Hawthorne evidence is weaker than commonly cited. The claim likely holds but the magnitude and mechanism in consulting contexts remain unverified.

## Claim 2: "Pattern recognition provides hypotheses, not automatic prescriptions"

**Exact proposition:** Naturalistic Decision Making (Gary Klein) shows expert pattern recognition is excellent, but organizational consulting differs from firefighting/EMS/chess in feedback timing and clarity.

**Embedded assumptions:**
- NDM/RPD demonstrates expert pattern recognition works well in certain domains
- Organizational consulting has different epistemic conditions than NDM's original domains (firefighting, EMS, military command, chess)
- The difference lies specifically in feedback timing and clarity
- Pattern recognition in consulting produces hypotheses requiring further testing

**Supporting evidence:**
- **NDM framework** [2]: Emerged to study how people make "cognitively complex functions in demanding, real-world situations" marked by "limited time, uncertainty, high stakes, team and organizational constraints, unstable conditions."
- **Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD) model** [2]: Three variations: (1) respond with first-generated course of action, (2) use mental simulation to understand course of events, (3) evaluate each course of action then choose. Critically, "expertise is crucial for using RPD."
- **NDM methodology** [2]: NDM studies "usually address the initial stages of observing phenomena and developing descriptive accounts" while "controlled laboratory studies emphasize the testing of hypotheses." This suggests NDM itself distinguishes between observation/model-building and hypothesis testing.
- **Complementarity** [2]: "NDM and controlled experimentation are thus complementary approaches. NDM provides the observations and models, and controlled experimentation provides the testing and formalization."

**Contrary evidence / Counterexamples:**
- **Domain transfer not established**: No direct evidence in NDM literature that pattern recognition fails or produces only hypotheses (rather than prescriptions) in organizational consulting specifically.
- **Expert intuition research**: Kahneman & Klein (2009, not retrieved) debated whether expert intuition is reliable; their agreement identified environmental regularity and opportunity to learn as key conditions—organizational consulting may have sufficient regularity for some pattern recognition.
- **Fast and frugal heuristics**: Gigerenzer's work (not retrieved) shows simple heuristics can outperform complex analysis in certain environments, suggesting pattern recognition might sometimes be prescriptive.

**Boundary conditions:**
- NDM domains (firefighting, EMS, military) have: immediate feedback, clear success/failure criteria, high repetition of similar situations, physical constraints.
- Organizational consulting has: delayed feedback (months/years), ambiguous success criteria, unique engagements, social/political constraints.
- The claim holds more strongly when feedback loops are >6 months and success criteria are contested.

**Competing explanations:**
1. **Ecological rationality** [3]: Pattern recognition may be prescriptive in environments with appropriate structure; consulting environments may lack that structure.
2. **Bounded rationality** [3]: Experts satisfice rather than optimize; pattern recognition may produce "good enough" prescriptions, not hypotheses.
3. **Bias and heuristics** [3]: Pattern recognition may encode systematic biases rather than genuine expertise.

**What would falsify:**
- Evidence that organizational consultants with 10+ years experience show high accuracy (>80%) in initial pattern-based diagnoses confirmed by later outcomes.
- Demonstration that organizational environments have sufficient regularity and feedback for RPD to produce reliable prescriptions.
- Studies showing NDM experts in consulting-like domains produce prescriptions, not hypotheses.

**Residual unknowns:**
- **No direct studies**: No NDM studies specifically examining organizational consultants as domain experts.
- **Feedback characteristics**: Unknown what feedback delay/ambiguity threshold separates "hypothesis-generating" from "prescription-generating" pattern recognition.
- **Expertise development**: Unknown whether consultants can develop RPD expertise comparable to firefighters given different feedback structure.

**Verdict:** **Plausible but untested.** NDM literature supports expert pattern recognition in high-feedback domains. The extension to organizational consulting is reasonable but lacks direct empirical support. The feedback timing/clarity distinction is theoretically sound but not empirically verified.

## Claim 3: "Experience entitles you to better hypotheses, not certainty"

**Exact proposition:** Experience in organizational/technical consulting improves hypothesis quality but does not produce certainty about outcomes or prescriptions.

**Embedded assumptions:**
- Experience improves something (hypothesis generation)
- Experience does not produce certainty
- The domain (organizational/technical consulting) is one where certainty is not achievable regardless of experience
- "Better hypotheses" is a meaningful, measurable category

**Supporting evidence:**
- **Bounded rationality** [3]: Simon's core argument: "replace the global rationality of economic man with the kind of rational behavior that is compatible with the access to information and the computational capacities that are actually possessed by organisms." This directly supports the claim that certainty is not achievable for cognitively limited agents.
- **Limits to logical omniscience** [3]: "Most formal models of judgement and decision making assume logical omniscience... This assumption is both psychologically unrealistic and technically challenging to avoid." Supports the claim that certainty requires impossible computational capacities.
- **Procedural rationality** [3]: Simon distinguished procedural rationality (effective given cognitive limits) from substantive rationality (optimal outcomes). Experience improves procedure, not necessarily certainty.
- **Satisficing** [3]: Experts use satisficing strategies, not optimization—suggests experience produces "good enough" not "certain."
- **Cumulative Prospect Theory** [3]: Kahneman & Tversky show systematic deviations from expected utility even among experts, suggesting experience doesn't eliminate uncertainty.

**Contrary evidence / Counterexamples:**
- **Expert intuition debate**: Kahneman & Klein (2009, not retrieved) identified conditions where expert intuition is reliable: (1) high-validity environment, (2) adequate opportunity to learn regularities. Some consulting domains might meet these criteria.
- **Fast and frugal heuristics** [3]: "Less is more effects" section notes simple heuristics can outperform complex analysis in specific environments—suggests experience might produce reliable (near-certain) prescriptions in some domains.
- **Proper vs. improper linear models** [3]: Dawes (1979) found even improper linear models outperform clinical intuition—but this suggests statistical models, not experience, produce better predictions.

**Boundary conditions:**
- Holds strongly in: novel situations, complex multi-variable systems, domains with delayed/ambiguous feedback.
- May not hold in: highly routine consulting tasks (e.g., standard compliance audits), domains with immediate clear feedback.
- Experience likely improves: cue selection, pattern recognition speed, hypothesis generation efficiency.
- Experience likely doesn't improve: prediction accuracy in inherently uncertain systems.

**Competing explanations:**
1. **Ecological rationality** [3]: Experience produces certainty in high-structure environments; consulting is low-structure.
2. **Bias accumulation**: Experience might entrench biases rather than improve hypotheses.
3. **Overconfidence**: Experienced consultants may express more certainty without better accuracy (not directly addressed in retrieved sources).

**What would falsify:**
- Longitudinal studies showing experienced consultants (>10 years) achieve >90% prediction accuracy on interventions.
- Evidence that certain organizational interventions produce reliable, predictable outcomes across contexts.
- Demonstration that "better hypotheses" doesn't correlate with experience level.

**Residual unknowns:**
- **No direct studies**: No studies measuring hypothesis quality vs. experience in consulting.
- **Definition of "better"**: Unclear whether "better hypotheses" means more accurate, more actionable, more testable, or something else.
- **Experience measurement**: Unknown whether years, number of engagements, or diversity of contexts best measures relevant experience.

**Verdict:** **Strongly supported by bounded rationality literature.** Simon's framework directly supports the claim that cognitively limited agents cannot achieve certainty. The extension to consulting is reasonable. The specific claim about "better hypotheses" lacks direct empirical support but is consistent with procedural rationality.

## Claim 4: "Some relational state is fundamentally inaccessible to outsiders"

**Exact proposition:** There are organizational states (particularly relational/interpersonal) that cannot be known by external consultants regardless of method or duration.

**Embedded assumptions:**
- Organizations have hidden or inaccessible states
- These states are relational/interpersonal in nature
- The inaccessibility is fundamental, not just practical (not solvable by more time or better methods)
- Outsiders (consultants) are particularly disadvantaged vs. insiders

**Supporting evidence:**
- **Sensemaking literature** [4]: Weick's seven properties include "identity and identification is central—who people think they are in their context shapes what they interpret." Identity is internal and may not be fully expressible.
- **Plausibility over accuracy** [4]: "People favour plausibility over accuracy in accounts of events and contexts... in an equivocal, postmodern world, infused with the politics of interpretation and inhabited by people with multiple shifting identities, an obsession with accuracy seems fruitless." This suggests some states may be unknowable even to insiders.
- **Bank wiring room experiments** [1]: Workers gave false responses to researchers, developed informal cliques with hidden rules—suggests some organizational states are actively concealed from observers.
- **Sensehiding** [4]: Literature identifies "sensehiding" as a related concept—deliberate concealment of meaning.

**Contrary evidence / Counterexamples:**
- **No direct evidence for fundamental inaccessibility**: Retrieved sources don't establish that states are *fundamentally* inaccessible, only that they're difficult to access or that people favor plausibility over accuracy.
- **Ethnographic methods**: Long-term participant observation (not addressed in retrieved sources) might access states unavailable to short-term consultants.
- **Insider limitations**: Insiders may also lack access to certain states (e.g., executive decision-making, parallel organizational processes).

**Boundary conditions:**
- May hold for: tacit knowledge, politically sensitive information, identity-level commitments, informal power structures.
- May not hold for: formal processes, documented decisions, observable behaviors.
- Time factor: Unknown whether "fundamentally inaccessible" applies after months/years of engagement vs. weeks.

**Competing explanations:**
1. **Social desirability bias**: States are accessible but people don't report them accurately.
2. **Power dynamics**: States are accessible but revealing them carries risk.
3. **Epistemic limits**: All knowledge is partial; this isn't specific to organizational consulting.
4. **Methodological limitations**: Better methods (longer engagement, different approaches) might access currently inaccessible states.

**What would falsify:**
- Evidence that long-term embedded consultants (6+ months) gain access to all organizationally relevant states.
- Demonstration that insiders have no better access than outsiders to relational states.
- Studies showing "fundamentally inaccessible" states become accessible with specific methods.

**Residual unknowns:**
- **No direct studies**: No empirical research on what organizational states are fundamentally vs. practically inaccessible.
- **Definition of "relational state"**: Unclear what counts as a relational state requiring access.
- **Insider vs. outsider**: Unknown whether insiders have systematically better access or just different blind spots.

**Verdict:** **Plausible but philosophically loaded.** Sensemaking literature supports the idea that meaning is constructed and favor plausibility over accuracy. The Bank wiring experiments show concealment. But "fundamentally inaccessible" is a strong epistemic claim not directly supported by retrieved sources. The claim may be more about practical limits framed as fundamental.

## Claim 5: "Act proportionally to reversibility, consequence, and learning velocity"

**Exact proposition:** Decision-making should calibrate action to three variables: how reversible the decision is, how significant the consequences are, and how fast learning occurs.

**Embedded assumptions:**
- These three variables are the right ones for proportionality
- Proportionality is a coherent decision criterion
- The framework is actionable (consultants can assess these variables)
- This aligns with or extends established decision theory

**Supporting evidence:**
- **Bounded rationality** [3]: Simon's framework emphasizes matching decision procedures to available information and computational capacity—consistent with calibrating action to context.
- **Satisficing** [3]: Setting aspiration levels based on context is consistent with proportional action.
- **Ecological rationality** [3]: "What may be an irrational decision strategy in one environment may be entirely rational in another"—supports context-sensitive decision making.
- **Bias-variance tradeoff** [3]: Different decision strategies appropriate in different environments.

**Contrary evidence / Counterexamples:**
- **No direct match in decision theory**: Retrieved sources don't contain this specific three-variable framework.
- **Expected utility theory** [3]: Standard decision theory uses probability × utility, not reversibility/consequence/learning velocity.
- **Missing variables**: Established frameworks include additional variables (probability, stakeholder preferences, ethical constraints) not captured in the three-variable framework.

**Boundary conditions:**
- Framework works best for: iterative interventions, learning-oriented contexts, reversible decisions.
- May not apply to: one-shot decisions, ethical absolutes, crisis situations requiring immediate action.

**Competing explanations:**
1. **Expected utility**: Standard framework is probability × utility; the three-variable framework may be a heuristic approximation.
2. **Real options theory**: Reversibility is central to real options thinking; consequence and learning velocity are less emphasized.
3. **OODA loop**: Military decision theory emphasizes observe-orient-decide-act with learning velocity implicit.

**What would falsify:**
- Evidence that decisions made using this framework perform worse than expected utility or other established frameworks.
- Demonstration that one of the three variables is irrelevant to decision quality.
- Studies showing additional variables are necessary for the framework to work.

**Residual unknowns:**
- **No empirical validation**: No studies testing this specific framework.
- **Operationalization**: Unclear how to measure "learning velocity" or "reversibility" in practice.
- **Tradeoffs**: Unknown how to weight variables when they point in different directions.

**Verdict:** **Reasonable synthesis but not established.** The framework is consistent with bounded and ecological rationality but is not directly supported by retrieved decision theory sources. It appears to be a pragmatic synthesis rather than an empirically validated model.

### 6b: Sensemaking (Karl Weick)

**Exact proposition:** Paper 1 overlaps with Weick's sensemaking framework.

**Embedded assumptions:**
- Weick's sensemaking addresses how people construct meaning in organizations
- Paper 1's claims about discovery, intervention, and inaccessible states align with sensemaking

**Supporting evidence:**
- **Sensemaking properties** [4]: Seven properties include identity, retrospection, enactment, social process, ongoing, cue extraction, plausibility over accuracy.
- **From decision-making to sensemaking** [4]: Weick shifted focus from decisions to meaning-making—aligns with Paper 1's emphasis on discovery as intervention.
- **Uncertainty and crisis** [4]: Sensemaking literature addresses uncertain, ambiguous situations—aligns with Paper 1's uncertainty claims.

**Contrary evidence:**
- **Different vantage point**: Paper 1 is from consultant-entry perspective; sensemaking is from insider/organizational perspective.

**Verdict:** **Strong overlap confirmed.** Paper 1's claims align with sensemaking literature, particularly around meaning construction, plausibility over accuracy, and enactment.

### 6c: Bounded Rationality (Herbert Simon)

**Exact proposition:** Paper 1 overlaps with Simon's bounded rationality.

**Embedded assumptions:**
- Simon's bounded rationality addresses cognitive limits in decision-making
- Paper 1's claims about experience and certainty align with bounded rationality

**Supporting evidence:**
- **Core argument** [3]: "Replace the global rationality of economic man with the kind of rational behavior that is compatible with the access to information and the computational capacities that are actually possessed by organisms."
- **Logical omniscience limits** [3]: Most models assume logical omniscience, which is "psychologically unrealistic."
- **Procedural rationality** [3]: Focus on effective procedures given limits, not optimal outcomes.

**Verdict:** **Strong overlap confirmed.** Paper 1's "experience entitles you to better hypotheses, not certainty" is a direct application of bounded rationality to consulting.

## Overall Assessment

**Claims that survived pressure testing:**
- Claim 3 (experience → hypotheses, not certainty): Strongest support from bounded rationality literature
- Claim 6b/6c (sensemaking/bounded rationality overlap): Clear theoretical alignment

**Claims that need qualification:**
- Claim 1 (discovery = intervention): Hawthorne effect weaker than commonly cited; mechanism unclear
- Claim 2 (pattern recognition → hypotheses): Reasonable extension of NDM but no direct evidence
- Claim 4 (inaccessible states): "Fundamental" inaccessibility not established; may be practical limits
- Claim 5 (proportionality framework): Reasonable but not empirically validated

**Claims requiring further research:**
- Claim 6a/6d (Deming, psychological safety): Not directly retrieved; overlap asserted but not verified

**Key gaps:**
- No direct studies of organizational consultants as domain experts
- No empirical validation of the three-variable proportionality framework
- No clear operationalization of "fundamentally inaccessible" states
- Hawthorne effect literature weaker than claim assumes
