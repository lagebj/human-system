# "It Depends" Paper Series — Working Map

**Status:** Working draft — not for publication  
**Created:** 2026-08-31

---

## The Recurring Question

> "It depends" is not the end of an answer. It is the beginning of the investigation into what the answer depends upon.

This series explores what this stance means for consulting, technical work, organizational change, decision-making, and intervention in human systems.

---

## Central Proposition (Under Test)

Experience provides pattern recognition and better hypotheses. It does not remove the need to understand the current context. Similarity between situations is not equivalence. The correct intervention depends on the conditions actually present, not merely on the category assigned to the problem.

**Key tension:** A consultant or practitioner is expected to know things. Experience has value. But experience becomes dangerous when "I have seen something that resembles this" silently becomes "I therefore know what this is."

---

## Reasoning Movement

The core inquiry pattern: it depends → identify what it depends upon → name the variables → distinguish which materially change the answer → state the current best explanation → name assumptions explicitly → act → observe → update.

This is not a framework. It is a recurring pattern of inquiry. The revised Paper 1 expresses this as prose rather than a numbered sequence.

## Paper 1: What Does Experience Actually Entitle Us to Assume?

**Provisional title:** *It Depends: What Experience Entitles Us to Assume*

**Central question:** When should experience increase confidence, and when should it increase the quality of our questions?

**Status:** Revised draft complete (2026-08-31). Working material, not publication-ready.

**Current proposition:**
- Experience provides pattern recognition, better hypotheses, faster orientation, knowledge of recurring failure modes, and sharper questions
- Pattern resemblance is not equivalence
- The danger appears when recognition substitutes for investigation
- A stronger stance: "I have seen things that resemble this. My experience helps me identify what to investigate, but the current context determines which similarities actually matter"

**Key territory:**
- The consultant enters an existing system (history, relationships, incentives, scars, workarounds, existing theories about what is wrong)
- Discovery is already intervention
- Acting under incomplete knowledge (we often need to decide before we have full understanding)
- Reversibility and consequence should affect evidence thresholds
- Allowing reality to update the model

**Strongest counterarguments to address:**
- Experts often make excellent rapid judgments (naturalistic decision making literature)
- Some patterns genuinely do travel safely across contexts
- Excessive emphasis on context can become an excuse for avoiding decisions
- Time pressure sometimes requires immediate action

**Overlap assessment:**
- May absorb "It Depends: Expertise" (likely the same argument)
- May absorb "The Consultant Is Part of the System" (likely a section, not a separate paper)

**Research needed (unverified claims):**
- Naturalistic Decision Making (Klein, Gary) — does NDM research support the hypothesis vs. prescription distinction?
- Deming's System of Profound Knowledge — verify overlap claims, distinguish what Deming establishes vs. what Human System infers
- Sensemaking (Weick) — verify mutual sensemaking claims
- Bounded Rationality (Simon) — verify proportionality framing extends bounded rationality
- Psychological Safety (Edmondson) — verify consultant-specific claims
- Observer effect in social/organizational systems — locate primary sources
- Decision theory on evidence thresholds — reversibility/consequence/learning velocity framework
- Expertise and pattern recognition literature
- Bounded rationality (Simon)
- Sensemaking (Weick)
- Deming overlap (System of Profound Knowledge — knowledge about variation, theory of knowledge)

## Future Paper Briefs

### Paper 2: It Depends — Best Practice

**Central question:** When does accumulated experience become a substitute for understanding the conditions in front of us?

**Territory:** Best practices, reference architectures, standard operating models, maturity models, playbooks

**Key distinction:** Not reflexively "best practices are bad." The interesting question is when they travel safely and when they do not.

**Overlap:** May connect to Paper 3 (Control) and Paper 4 (Standardization)

---

### Paper 3: It Depends — Control

**Central question:** What happens when attempts to improve visibility change what people are willing to reveal?

**Territory:** The loop: more control → greater perceived consequence → more defensive behavior → less truthful information → worse observability → worse decisions → more control

**Counterpressure:** Controls are not inherently bad. Some controls genuinely improve safety and coordination.

**Research needed:**
- Control theory
- Safety science (Sidney Dekker, David Woods)
- Goodhart's Law / Campbell's Law
- Psychological safety (Amy Edmondson)

---

### Paper 4: It Depends — Standardization

**Central question:** Which variation should we remove, and which variation contains information about the system?

**Territory:** Useful standardization versus destruction of local sensing and judgment

**Counterpressure:** Standardization often materially improves performance. Some variation is merely noise.

---

### Paper 5: It Depends — Architecture

**Central question:** When does the architecture diagram stop describing the system we actually operate?

**Territory:** Abstractions are necessary. The danger is confusing the abstraction with the system.

**Likely approach:** Technical examples where the lived system diverged from the documented architecture

---

### Paper 6: It Depends — Who Was in the Room?

**Central question:** How much of an organizational decision can be understood without understanding who participated, who did not, and what relationships existed between them?

**Territory:** Relationships as living infrastructure. Trust, resentment, admiration, obligation, fear, affection, rivalry, embarrassment, status, private interpretations, shared history.

**Key proposition:** Some relational state may be unknowable to an observer. Even a relationship graph is still an abstraction.

## Unresolved Tensions

1. **Novelty vs. overlap:** How much of this is genuinely distinctive vs. rediscovering established work (Deming, systems thinking, socio-technical systems)?

2. **Consultant exceptionalism:** The consultant perspective is an entrypoint. But are these dynamics actually broader? Avoid implying consultants uniquely experience these dynamics.

3. **Infinite context:** Complete understanding is impossible. How much discovery is enough? When is rapid pattern recognition genuinely superior?

4. **Relativism:** "It depends" must not imply that every answer is equally valid. Some interventions are better supported than others.

5. **Anti-standardization reflex:** Contextual reasoning is not an argument against all standards, controls, or patterns.

## Falsification / Revision Conditions

The central proposition would need revision if:

1. Research shows expert pattern recognition is reliable across significantly different contexts
2. Best-practice transfer succeeds more often than contextual adaptation
3. Standardization consistently outperforms local judgment
4. Control consistently improves (rather than degrades) information quality
5. "It depends" provides no actionable guidance beyond "context matters"
