# Initial Voice Observations

> Provisional. Derived from 38 direct user messages (1986 approximate words) across several surfaces. These are hypotheses to test as the corpus grows, not generation rules.

## Cross-surface signals

### Ordinary conjunctions carry a lot of thought

The seed repeatedly uses ordinary connective language instead of formal transitions.

Observed raw counts in this small corpus:

- `and`: 61
- `but`: 11
- `because`: 2
- `so`: 4
- `if`: 12
- `when`: 4
- `also`: 12
- `maybe`: 3
- `think`: 8
- `not`: 24
- `do`: 14
- `we`: 42
- `i`: 39
- `should`: 18
- `could`: 7
- `really`: 4

Never use these as target frequencies.

### Qualification often arrives inside or after a position

The author frequently commits first, then complicates:

- `I fully agree ... Another thing ...`
- `I think ... as well`
- `maybe?`
- a direct preference followed by a boundary or exception.

This differs from front-loading every statement with cautious hedge language.

### Long accumulation is often cognitive work

Several samples continue adding variables, examples, or conditions until the distinction becomes visible. Cleaning these into taxonomies can destroy the movement.

### Compression also belongs

The corpus contains both long accumulating reasoning and extreme compression:

`People and their feelings and reactions are also infra`

Do not infer a single preferred sentence length.

### Repetition can be search

Repeated words and near-synonyms often appear when one term is not enough:

- `"AI"`, `"processed"`, `"polished"`
- repeated `it depends`
- repeated `who`, `what`, `where`
- repeated state words such as `lock`, `finalise`, `complete`

Do not automatically remove repetition.

### Questions are part of reasoning

Questions often appear mid-thought:

- `do we really need all of these?`
- `is for example OPA useful here?`
- `if you understand what I mean?`

They are usually not engagement hooks.

### Concrete examples often do more than abstract labels

Technical reasoning commonly moves through actual numbers, situations, user actions, and exceptions rather than abstract categories.

### Direct correction is normal

The author often says the disagreement first, then explains:

- `No, ...`
- `I think ...`
- `Do not ...`
- `we cannot solely depend on that ...`

The correction is usually practical rather than ceremonially softened.

### Dry humour can emerge by literal continuation

The `It depends` passage moves from serious decision variables into colour and shape without announcing a joke. The humour comes from continuing to take the claim seriously.

### Shorthand is natural when context is shared

Examples include `infra`, `IMO`, `X amount`, `org`, and domain-specific abbreviations without explanation.

Public writing may need more context than chat, but expanding every shorthand into professional abstraction will erase some voice.

## Surface differences already visible

### Reflective/conceptual

More accumulation, repetition, explicit first-person uncertainty, and broad associative movement.

### Technical reasoning

Concrete examples, numeric cases, constraints, exceptions, questions, and implementation consequences. Often moves directly from problem to possible rule to edge case.

### Argument/correction

Shorter entry, direct disagreement or instruction, then concrete boundary.

### Meta-writing feedback

Highly compressed judgments using repeated near-synonyms, quotation marks, and explicit emotional reaction to prose quality.

## Gaps

This seed is still conversation-heavy.

High-value future additions:

- long author-original email;
- older pre-generative-AI technical writing;
- reflective writing composed without assistant scaffolding;
- disagreement with peers;
- explanation written for an unfamiliar audience;
- natural humour in a longer piece;
- calm prose written after the thinking had already matured.
