# Public Language for Human System

This document defines the authorship model for reader-facing Human System material that is intentionally published. It is distinct from the manuscript voice and from ordinary operational communication.

Human System is a body of thought in public. The writing should reflect how the author actually thinks without turning provisional observation into polished framework or manufactured certainty.

## Core stance

Public Human System writing sounds like a technically and organisationally experienced person thinking about something they have noticed and consider worth sharing.

The voice should feel:

- grounded in actual experience
- curious rather than declarative
- reflective without becoming ornate
- plain-spoken
- comfortable with ambiguity
- willing to take a position
- willing to change that position
- serious without becoming solemn
- intelligent without performing intelligence

First-person language is valid when the claim belongs to the author. Do not hide judgment behind fake institutional neutrality.

## Epistemic stance

Uncertainty is a first-class part of the writing model. Do not treat it as something to clean up during publication preparation.

Distinguish naturally among:

- what I have directly experienced
- what I have observed repeatedly
- what I currently believe
- what seems plausible
- what I suspect
- what I am extrapolating
- what research supports
- what research merely resembles
- what I do not know
- what appears contradictory
- what would cause me to reconsider
- what depends heavily on context

Natural epistemic language includes:

- "I think..."
- "I keep coming back to..."
- "What I have seen..."
- "My current view is..."
- "I am less certain about..."
- "I don't know yet."
- "I suspect..."
- "This seems to depend on..."
- "I have seen enough of this to take it seriously, although I am not sure I understand all of it yet."
- "I may be wrong about the mechanism, but the pattern is difficult for me to ignore."

These are examples of stance, not templates. Do not reproduce them automatically.

A piece may end with an unresolved question. A page may describe a tension without resolving it. A paper may expose two plausible interpretations without manufacturing a false synthesis.

Editorial maturity does not increase epistemic certainty. Avoid upgrading `observation -> hypothesis -> principle -> law` merely because the writing has been revised.

## Sentence construction

Let sentence length follow the thought. Variation should emerge naturally.

Short sentences are allowed. Long sentences are allowed. A short sentence should not automatically become a punchline. A long sentence should not exist merely because a style guide says long prose feels thoughtful.

Use ordinary connective words freely: `and`, `but`, `so`, `because`, `while`, `although`.

Avoid excessive noun-heavy consultant language. Prefer:

> people start waiting for approval

over:

> an increase in approval-path dependency emerges

when the former says what actually happened.

Do not polish every irregularity away. Slight roughness, repetition of an ordinary word, or an asymmetrical sentence may be more authentic than a perfectly balanced alternative.

## Paragraphs

Public web writing and manuscript writing are different surfaces. Do not inherit the manuscript requirement that long paragraphs are the default or that skimmability is inherently suspicious.

At the same time, do not turn public pages into a stack of one- or two-sentence content-marketing paragraphs.

A paragraph should contain a real movement of thought. Break when the footing changes. Do not break merely to create visual rhythm. Do not merge merely to satisfy a prose rule.

Some pages should be denser than others. Structural irregularity is healthy.

## Lists and bullets

Lists are not prohibited. Listification is.

Use a list when the material is genuinely an inventory, sequence, set of conditions, collection of examples, references, options, or items that benefit from comparison.

Do not convert reasoning into bullets merely because the agent identified several related ideas.

**Core rule:** Lists are for things that behave like lists. Prose is for thinking.

Avoid automatically producing:

- three principles
- four lessons
- five takeaways
- key benefits
- key challenges
- "what this means"
- "why this matters"
- "how to apply it"

unless the source material genuinely calls for that form.

Do not optimise page structure for content-marketing conventions.

## Headings

Headings should correspond to meaningful changes in subject or footing. Do not generate a heading every two or three paragraphs.

Do not automatically use headings such as:

- Why this matters
- Key takeaways
- The bigger picture
- What this means
- How it works
- Final thoughts
- In conclusion

These are not forbidden strings. They are warning signs when generated reflexively.

Do not make all pages structurally identical. A note, essay, hypothesis, pattern, paper, and research-backed argument do not need the same page skeleton.

## Humour, irony, and contrast

The author's public voice includes dry humour. It may occasionally include understated sarcasm, irony, absurd comparison, or an opposite comparison that exposes a contradiction.

Preserve this possibility. Do not turn humour into a quota or stylistic requirement.

Humour should generally be:

- dry
- brief
- situational
- slightly underplayed
- aimed at an absurd idea, process, incentive, contradiction, or organisational behaviour rather than at an individual person
- allowed to sit without explaining the joke

Sarcasm may be sharper occasionally, but should not make the narrator sound superior to the people inside the system. The author is part of these systems too.

Do not ban contrastive constructions globally. Use opposites, contrast, reversal, irony, counterexample, or an intentionally absurd comparison when they arise naturally.

The problem is pattern repetition, not the existence of contrast.

## Metaphor and comparison

Metaphor is welcome when it clarifies how the author sees something. Do not decorate prose with generic journeys, landscapes, ecosystems, orchestras, tapestries, bridges, north stars, or similar stock images unless they genuinely belong to the thought.

One useful metaphor can carry a piece. Five metaphors usually mean the prose is trying too hard.

Opposite comparison can be especially useful when it exposes the gap between what a system claims and what people experience. Use it naturally rather than building a rhetorical template around it.

## Closure

Do not automatically close. Do not assume every artifact requires:

- a conclusion
- recommendations
- lessons learned
- action items
- a summary
- a call to action

Stop when the thought is done. The final paragraph may:

- leave a question
- expose a contradiction
- return to an observation
- state what remains uncertain
- stop at the edge of the author's current understanding

Do not append:

- "Ultimately..."
- "At its core..."
- "The key takeaway..."
- "The real lesson..."
- "In conclusion..."

simply because the model expects an ending.

## Relationship to evidence

Preserve a clear distinction between:

- personal observation
- interpretation
- hypothesis
- external evidence
- established theory
- speculation

External evidence may strengthen, challenge, complicate, or bound the author's thinking. It must not be used as decoration around a pre-decided argument.

When research does not settle the question, say so. When research supports only part of the proposition, preserve that boundary. When personal experience is the basis for a claim, do not convert it into a universal statement merely because similar literature exists.

## Advice and prescription

Public Human System material is allowed to contain practical implications. Unlike the manuscript, public material is not globally prohibited from giving advice.

However, implication should not automatically become prescription. A useful progression is often:

> observation -> interpretation -> possible implication

without forcing:

> observation -> rule -> framework -> checklist

Advice must reflect context and uncertainty. "It depends" is allowed, but it must lead somewhere useful:

- depends on what?
- which variables matter?
- what appears true here?
- what could safely be assumed?
- what would make the intervention inappropriate?
- what happened when it was tried?

Do not package context-sensitive thinking into universally applicable practices simply to make the artifact more useful-looking.

## AI-shaped prose patterns to guard against

Do not implement an "AI humanizer." Do not introduce an AI-writing score. Do not use an AI detector as a quality gate. Do not create a simple banned-word list.

Instead, guard against repeated generative tendencies such as:

- overly balanced structures
- repeated binary correction
- excessive triads
- exhaustive coverage
- artificial completeness
- constant summarisation
- repeated section templates
- repeated sentence shapes
- excessive abstract vocabulary
- inflated language
- content-marketing cadence
- every section finishing with a neat conclusion
- every page explaining its own importance
- every thought being turned into advice
- every uncertainty being resolved
- decorative metaphors added simply to make prose feel literary
- an agent outline remaining visible in the final artifact

The test is not "Could a human have written this?" The stronger question is:

> Does this feel like this author had something specific to say here?

## What this is not

This is not:

- a template for assembling pages
- a content-marketing style guide
- an AI humanization system
- a vocabulary blacklist
- a readability score requirement
- a mandate for constant hedging
- a requirement for humour
- a framework for turning thinking into methodology

Different ideas deserve different shapes. Provide heuristics, not a prose assembly line.

## Final integrity check

Before accepting public prose, ask:

> Does this feel like a person exploring a view or an organisation declaring policy?

> Where did the writer appear more certain than the evidence justified?

> Did any list or heading structure feel mechanical?

> What appears unresolved? Did unresolved material feel intentional or merely incomplete?

> What do I think the author's own position is?

> Did any humour or sarcasm alter how I interpreted the author's stance?

If the text sounds prepared, explanatory, or rhetorically shaped, it is suspect.
